Know, Detect, Prevent

A detailed medical guide to the most commonly diagnosed types of cancer worldwide. Education and prevention are our best allies for health.

Breast Cervix Prostate Lung Stomach Colorectal

Breast Cancer

What is it?

Breast cancer is a disease in which cells in the breast tissue mutate and multiply uncontrollably, usually giving rise to a malignant tumor. It can originate either in the milk ducts (ductal carcinoma, the most common type) or in the glandular lobules (lobular carcinoma). Although it mainly affects women from their forties onward, men can also develop it in a smaller percentage of cases (about 1%).

Risk factors
  • Advanced age: Risk increases significantly from age 40 to 50 onward.
  • Genetics and family history: Inherited mutations in the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes, or direct family history of breast or ovarian cancer.
  • Hormonal and reproductive factors: Early menarche (before age 12) or late menopause (after age 55), as well as prolonged exposure to hormone replacement therapy.
  • Lifestyle: Frequent alcohol consumption, being overweight or obese after menopause, and a sedentary lifestyle.
  • Radiological factors: A history of radiation therapy to the chest area during youth.
Warning symptoms
  • A palpable lump, mass, or thickening in the breast or underarm area, usually painless but firm.
  • Noticeable changes in the shape, size, or symmetry of the breasts.
  • Changes in the skin covering the breast, such as dimpling, puckering, an "orange peel" appearance, or unexplained redness.
  • Nipple abnormalities, such as recent retraction (inversion), spontaneous discharge (especially if bloody), or persistent scaling.
Detection and prevention

A comprehensive early-detection strategy includes monthly breast self-exams starting at age 20 to learn the normal structure of the breast, annual clinical exams by a professional, and regular mammograms starting at age 40. As primary prevention measures, it's recommended to maintain a healthy body weight, exercise regularly, limit or avoid alcohol consumption, and prioritize a diet rich in antioxidants and fiber.

Cervical Cancer

What is it?

Also known as cervical cancer, it originates in the cells of the cervix (the lower part of the uterus that connects to the vagina). Its development is usually very slow, starting with precancerous lesions known as dysplasia. It's directly and almost exclusively linked to persistent infection by oncogenic types of Human Papillomavirus (HPV), with genotypes 16 and 18 responsible for the majority of serious cases.

Risk factors
  • Persistent HPV infection: Contracting high oncogenic-risk strains that the immune system fails to clear over time.
  • Sexual behavior: Early onset of sexual activity and a history of multiple sexual partners (which increases the likelihood of exposure to the virus).
  • Smoking: Smoking doubles the risk of cervical cancer, since chemicals in smoke weaken the local immune system of cervical tissue.
  • Weakened immune system: Conditions such as HIV/AIDS or prolonged use of immunosuppressant medications.
  • Multiple pregnancies: Having had multiple full-term pregnancies without frequent gynecological check-ups.
Warning symptoms
  • Abnormal, unexpected vaginal bleeding, such as bleeding after intercourse, bleeding between regular periods, or bleeding after menopause.
  • Unusual vaginal discharge, which may become persistent, watery, thick, or streaked with blood and have a foul odor.
  • Constant pelvic pain, discomfort, or a feeling of heaviness in the lower abdomen.
  • Discomfort or sharp pain during intercourse (dyspareunia).
Detection and prevention

The Pap smear (vaginal cytology) and molecular tests for HPV DNA detection are highly effective tools capable of identifying lesions before they become malignant. Primary prevention includes preventive HPV vaccination given to preadolescents before the start of sexual activity, correct and consistent use of barrier methods (condoms), and reducing tobacco use.

Prostate Cancer

What is it?

Prostate cancer develops in the prostate gland in men, a small walnut-shaped organ located below the bladder whose main function is to produce the seminal fluid that nourishes and transports sperm. It's one of the most commonly diagnosed tumors in the male population. The vast majority of cases are adenocarcinomas and are characterized by an extremely slow progression, which allows for a very favorable prognosis when diagnosed early.

Risk factors
  • Advancing age: Incidence increases exponentially from age 50 onward.
  • Family history: Having a father or brother diagnosed with prostate cancer doubles or triples personal risk.
  • Ethnic background: Men of African descent have a notably higher incidence and aggressiveness rate compared to other ethnic groups.
  • Diet and metabolism: Eating patterns high in saturated animal fats and low in fruits, vegetables, and lycopene, combined with a sedentary lifestyle.
Warning symptoms
  • Frequent urination problems, especially a slow, weak, or intermittent urine flow that stops and starts.
  • A sudden, urgent need to get up multiple times during the night to urinate (nocturia).
  • Presence of blood traces in urine or semen.
  • Persistent discomfort, stiffness, or pain localized in the lower back, hips, or upper thighs due to bone metastasis in advanced stages.
Detection and prevention

Preventive and early-diagnosis methods rely on an annual medical evaluation using the Prostate-Specific Antigen (PSA) blood test and a digital rectal exam starting at age 50 (or from age 45 if there's significant family history). It's recommended to promote a balanced diet rich in vegetables, cruciferous vegetables, and tomatoes, maintain a healthy body weight, and get regular medical check-ups for any urinary changes.

Lung Cancer

What is it?

Lung cancer originates in lung tissue, usually in the cells lining the airways and bronchi. It's mainly divided into two large groups: small cell lung cancer and non-small cell lung cancer. It's one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, strongly associated in an overwhelming majority of cases with long-term tobacco use and inhaled smoke byproducts.

Risk factors
  • Active tobacco use: Responsible for more than 85% of total cases due to the massive presence of carcinogenic chemical agents in smoke.
  • Secondhand smoke: Constant inhalation of ambient tobacco smoke generated by nearby smokers at home or work.
  • Radon gas exposure: A natural radioactive gas that accumulates indoors and in basements, originating from soil and rocks.
  • Occupational and industrial risks: Chronic inhalation of toxic substances such as asbestos, arsenic, beryllium, nickel, soot, and diesel emissions.
  • Air pollution: Exposure to high levels of fine particulate matter suspended in the air of large industrial cities.
Warning symptoms
  • A chronic cough that lingers over time, changes tone, or progressively worsens with no apparent infectious cause.
  • Coughing up blood or sputum tinged with reddish tones.
  • A constant feeling of shortness of breath (dyspnea), choking, or wheezing and sharp chest pain when breathing deeply.
  • Rapid, unexplained weight loss, chronic fatigue, generalized weakness, and recurring episodes of pneumonia or bronchitis.
Detection and prevention

The single most effective preventive measure is to completely avoid tobacco use and quit smoking as early as possible. For people with a high-risk smoking history (adults ages 50 to 80 with an extensive smoking history), medical protocols recommend annual preventive screening with low-dose computed tomography (LDCT), allowing nodules to be identified at very early stages.

Stomach Cancer

What is it?

Gastric (stomach) cancer develops when the cells lining the inner mucosa of the stomach begin to multiply abnormally, eventually forming a tumor lesion. Its progression tends to be slow and quiet over the years, usually preceded by chronic inflammatory processes such as atrophic gastritis. The predominant histological type is gastric adenocarcinoma, which responds positively to combined treatments if detected before invading deeper layers or nearby lymph nodes.

Risk factors
  • Helicobacter pylori infection: Chronic bacterial colonization of the stomach that damages the mucosa and promotes cellular mutations over the long term.
  • Dietary habits: Frequent intake of foods preserved with large amounts of salt, smoked or processed meats, and low intake of fresh fruits or vegetables rich in vitamin C.
  • Active smoking: Ingested smoke and its toxins substantially increase the risk of developing tumors in the gastric region.
  • Genetic history: Family history of gastric cancer or belonging to hereditary cancer-predisposition syndromes.
  • Prior conditions: Chronic atrophic gastritis, pernicious anemia, or previous stomach surgeries for benign ulcers.
Warning symptoms
  • Persistent indigestion, diffuse abdominal discomfort, chronic heartburn, or a constant burning sensation in the upper abdomen.
  • A feeling of fullness or early satiety after eating very small portions of food.
  • Sudden weight loss, loss of appetite, and sudden aversion to certain foods (such as red meat).
  • Frequent episodes of nausea, recurring vomiting, and dark stools (melena) or vomit with traces of blood.
Detection and prevention

Prevention focuses on timely medical eradication of the bacteria Helicobacter pylori through specific antibiotic treatments when detected, adopting a diet rich in fresh products, strictly reducing preservatives and salt, and avoiding tobacco use. In patients with high symptomatic risk or direct family history, an upper digestive endoscopy allows for a precise, early diagnosis.

Colorectal Cancer

What is it?

Colorectal cancer includes malignant tumors that develop in the colon or rectum, the final part of the digestive system. In most cases, it begins as a polyp, a benign growth of tissue on the intestinal wall that, over the years, can become cancerous if not detected and removed in time. It's one of the most preventable types of cancer, precisely because its precancerous stage (the polyp) is easily identified through endoscopic studies.

Risk factors
  • Age: Risk increases notably after age 50, although cases in younger people are on the rise.
  • Family and personal history: Previous polyps, inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis or Crohn's), or first-degree relatives with colorectal cancer.
  • Diet: Diets high in red and processed meats and low in fiber, fruits, and vegetables.
  • Lifestyle: Sedentary habits, obesity, smoking, and high alcohol consumption.
  • Type 2 diabetes: Associated with a moderately higher risk of developing this type of cancer.
Warning symptoms
  • Persistent changes in bowel habits, such as diarrhea, constipation, or altered stool consistency lasting several weeks.
  • Blood in the stool or rectal bleeding.
  • Continuous abdominal discomfort, such as cramping, gas, or pain that doesn't go away.
  • A feeling that the bowel doesn't empty completely, weakness, fatigue, and weight loss with no apparent cause.
Detection and prevention

Colonoscopy is the gold-standard tool, since it can detect and remove polyps before they become cancerous; it's recommended starting at age 45–50, or earlier if there's family history. Other complementary tests include the fecal occult blood test. Preventive measures include increasing fiber, fruit, and vegetable intake, reducing processed meats, staying physically active, and avoiding tobacco and excessive alcohol.

Early detection is the best tool against cancer. Always consult with a healthcare professional.

A general guide to the process — each case may vary depending on medical judgment

1

Initial consultation

You visit a general practitioner and explain the symptoms you've noticed.

2

Screening tests

Lab tests, imaging, or specific studies are ordered depending on the case.

3

Biopsy

If there's a suspicious lesion, a small tissue sample is taken for analysis.

4

Diagnosis and staging

The medical team confirms the diagnosis and determines what stage it's at.

5

Treatment plan

A personalized plan is defined together with oncology specialists.

Basic medical glossary

Terms that come up frequently when talking about cancer, explained in plain language

Benign tumor
A growth of cells that does not invade other tissues or spread to other parts of the body.
Malignant tumor
A growth of cells capable of invading nearby tissues and spreading to other areas of the body.
Metastasis
The process by which cancer cells break away from the original tumor and form new tumors in other parts of the body.
Biopsy
Removal of a small tissue sample to examine under a microscope and confirm whether it contains cancer cells.
Adenocarcinoma
A type of cancer that originates in glandular cells, found in organs such as the breast, prostate, lung, or stomach.
Chemotherapy
Treatment with medications aimed at destroying or slowing the growth of cancer cells in the body.
Radiation therapy
Use of high-energy radiation aimed at a specific area to destroy cancer cells or shrink tumors.
Screening
Tests performed on people without symptoms to detect possible signs of cancer at very early stages.
Remission
The decrease or disappearance of cancer signs and symptoms, either partially or completely.

This glossary is for informational purposes only and does not replace an explanation from a healthcare professional about your particular case.
